Combinatorial Auctions

Problem Description

In the Combinatorial Auction (CA) problem, \(n\) bids \((B_i, p_i)\) for subsets of \(m\) items are given, where \(B_i\) is the subset and \(p_i\) is its associated bidding price. The objective is to allocate items to bids to maximize the total revenue: \[\max \sum_{i\in[n]} p_ix_i \] \[\textrm{s.t.} \sum_{i: j\in B_i} x_i \leq 1, \,\forall j\in[m], \] \[ x_i\in\{0,1\}, \, \forall i\in[n].\] There are two commonly-used distributions: regions and arbitrary, introduced in [Leyton-Brown00].

Related Literature

Learning to Branch

For learning to branch, CA has been used as a benchmark in [Gasse19, Gupta20] where their generator could be found here. In addition, Ecole has a generator for CA instances that allows customized problem parameters.  For 1-hour runtime limit with SCIP, the typical problem sizes that have been used range from 100 items with 500 bids to 500 items with 1500 bids.

Large Neighborhood Search (LNS)

For large neighborhood search, CA has been used in [Song20, Wu21, Huang23]. For 1-hour runtime limit,  the typical problem sizes that have been used range from 2,000 items and 4,000 bids to 8,000 items and 16,000 bids. In addition to the distributions complied in Distributional MIPLIB shown in the table above, [Wu21] used CA instances with 8000 items and 16000 bids.

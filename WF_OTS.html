Wildfire-Aware Optimal Transmission Switching

Problem Description

The Wildfire-Aware Optimal Transmission Switching (WF_OTS) problem models the operational decisions underlying Public Safety Power Shutoffs (PSPS), in which utilities de-energize transmission lines in high-risk areas to mitigate wildfire ignition risk. Given the wildfire risk associated with each line, WF-OTS determines which lines to de-energize to minimize total load shedding across the network, with a small penalty on the number of de-energized lines. The aggregate wildfire risk of energized lines is constrained by a system-wide threshold. WF_OTS is formulated as a mixed-integer linear program (MILP) under a DC power flow approximation, with binary variables representing line energization decisions and continuous variables for generation dispatch, power flows, and load shedding. Big-M constraints enforce the DC power flow equations for energized lines while allowing de-energized lines to carry no power, alongside generator capacity limits and nodal power balance constraints. Each instance represents a single operating period with fixed wildfire risk levels, reflecting the short-term operational setting of PSPS decisions.

The MILP formulation for the WF_OTS instances in this library is a single-period version of the Optimal Power Shutoff (OPS) problem [Rhodes21, Piansky25], derived from [Piansky25] and used in [Huang27].

Instances Distribution

The table below summarizes the distributions that have been used in previous works, along with download links.  Instances from [Huang27] are generated using the California Test System (CATS) and daily USGS Wildland Fire Potential Index (WFPI) maps from 2020 and 2021, with each transmission line’s wildfire risk defined as the maximum WFPI value along its path. For each day, a representative hour is selected based on the maximum load shedding under a threshold-based shutoff policy. Days with no wildfire risk or load shedding are excluded. Only instances whose thresholded load shed is at least 100 MWh are used in the ML-guided experiments of [Huang27] . These are split randomly into training (80%, 432 instances), validation (10%, 54 instances), and test (10%, 54 instances) sets.
